# The four-day week, one year on

*Halden Work Institute · 26 March 2026*

A year ago, 48 companies agreed to try a four-day week with no cut in pay. We followed every one of
them for twelve months. This is what happened.

**The headline: 92% of the companies kept the four-day week after the trial ended.** Of the 48, 44
are still running it today. Three went back to five days. One closed for reasons its founders say had
nothing to do with the trial.

## What changed for staff

Staff reported less burnout across the board: 68% said they felt less burnt out at the end of the
year than at the start. Sick days fell by 41% compared with the year before. Fewer people left, too.
Resignations dropped by 37%, and several companies told us that hiring got easier once the policy was
in their job adverts.

## What changed for the business

The worry going in was output. It did not fall. Revenue across the group rose by 2.1% over the year,
in line with the companies' own forecasts made before the trial. Most teams cut meetings first: the
median team spent 6 fewer hours a week in them.

## What did not work

It was not easy for everyone. Customer support teams found it hardest, because customers still
expect an answer on the fifth day. The companies that made it work there used rotas, not a shared day
off. Two of the three that returned to five days said support was the reason.

## What we recommend

Start with a trial, measure before and after, and decide what "the same output" means before you
begin. The full report, with the data for every company, is free to download.
